# Day 4 — Hidden Markov Models

## 1. Theoretical foundations

### 1.1 The model

Sequential data where what you observe is generated by a hidden discrete state that evolves over time. Two assumptions:

**Markov property.** The next state depends only on the current one:
$$
P(q_t \mid q_{1:t-1}) = P(q_t \mid q_{t-1})
$$

**Conditional independence of emissions.** Given the state, the observation is independent of everything else:
$$
P(x_t \mid q_{1:T}, x_{1:T\setminus t}) = P(x_t \mid q_t)
$$

Parameters $\lambda = (A, B, \pi)$, exactly as in the 2026 exam:

| | shape | meaning | constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| $A$ | $h\times h$ | $A_{ij}=P(q_t=j\mid q_{t-1}=i)$ | rows sum to 1 |
| $B$ | $h\times d$ | $B_{jk}=P(x_t=k\mid q_t=j)$ | rows sum to 1 |
| $\pi$ | $h$ | $\pi_i=P(q_1=i)$ | sums to 1 |

Those row-sum conditions are literally Q5 part 1 of the 2026 exam. They're not bookkeeping — each row is a probability distribution over "where do I go next" / "what do I emit".

Joint likelihood of a state path and observations:
$$
P(x_{1:T}, q_{1:T}\mid\lambda) = \pi_{q_1}B_{q_1,x_1}\prod_{t=2}^{T}A_{q_{t-1},q_t}B_{q_t,x_t}
$$

### 1.2 The three problems (Rabiner 1989)

This trio is the backbone of every MC question on HMMs. Memorise the mapping:

| problem | question | algorithm |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Evaluation** | $P(x_{1:T}\mid\lambda)$ — how likely is this sequence? | **forward** |
| **2. Decoding** | $\arg\max_q P(q_{1:T}\mid x_{1:T})$ — what path did it take? | **Viterbi** |
| **3. Learning** | $\arg\max_\lambda P(x_{1:T}\mid\lambda)$ — fit the parameters | **Baum–Welch** (EM) |

The 2026 MC asked exactly about "the roles of the forward algorithm, the Viterbi algorithm, and the Baum–Welch algorithm". This table *is* the answer.

### 1.3 Forward algorithm

Naive evaluation sums over all $h^T$ paths — intractable. Dynamic programming exploits the Markov structure.

Define
$$
\alpha_t(i) := P(x_{1:t},\, q_t=i \mid \lambda)
$$
Read it carefully: joint probability of *the observations so far* **and** *being in state $i$ now*.

Initialisation:
$$
\alpha_1(i)=\pi_i B_{i,x_1}
$$
Recursion (marginalise over where you came from):
$$
\alpha_t(j)=\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{h}\alpha_{t-1}(i)A_{ij}\Big]B_{j,x_t}
$$
Termination:
$$
P(x_{1:T}\mid\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{h}\alpha_T(i)
$$

Cost $O(Th^2)$ instead of $O(h^T)$. In matrix form the recursion is one line: `alpha[t] = (alpha[t-1] @ A) * B[:, obs[t]]`.

**Derivation of the recursion** (worth being able to produce):
$$
\alpha_t(j)=P(x_{1:t},q_t=j)=\sum_i P(x_{1:t-1},q_{t-1}=i,q_t=j,x_t)
=\sum_i \underbrace{P(x_{1:t-1},q_{t-1}=i)}_{\alpha_{t-1}(i)}\underbrace{P(q_t=j\mid q_{t-1}=i)}_{A_{ij}}\underbrace{P(x_t\mid q_t=j)}_{B_{j,x_t}}
$$
using the Markov property in the second factor and conditional independence in the third.

### 1.4 Scaling — why it matters

$\alpha_t$ is a product of $2T$ probabilities. For $T=1000$ it underflows to zero in double precision. Fix: normalise at each step.
$$
c_t=\sum_i\hat\alpha_t(i),\qquad \hat\alpha_t=\alpha_t/c_t,\qquad
\log P(x_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log c_t
$$
The exam supplied the recursion in 2026, but *mentioning scaling* is free marks and shows you'd actually implement it.

### 1.5 Backward algorithm

$$
\beta_t(i):=P(x_{t+1:T}\mid q_t=i)
$$
Note the asymmetry with $\alpha$: this one is *conditional*, not joint. Initialise $\beta_T(i)=1$; recursion
$$
\beta_t(i)=\sum_j A_{ij}B_{j,x_{t+1}}\beta_{t+1}(j)
$$
Combined: $P(x_{1:T}) = \sum_i \alpha_t(i)\beta_t(i)$ for **any** $t$ — a useful correctness check when coding.

### 1.6 Viterbi

Same recursion with $\sum \to \max$, plus backpointers:
$$
\delta_t(j)=\Big[\max_i \delta_{t-1}(i)A_{ij}\Big]B_{j,x_t},
\qquad
\psi_t(j)=\arg\max_i \delta_{t-1}(i)A_{ij}
$$
Then backtrack from $\arg\max_i\delta_T(i)$.

**The distinction that MC questions probe:** forward gives the *probability summed over all paths*; Viterbi gives the *single most probable path*. And crucially, the Viterbi path is **not** the sequence of individually most-likely states — the latter (from $\gamma_t$, below) can contain transitions with $A_{ij}=0$, i.e. be an impossible path. Work in log domain to avoid underflow, since $\max$ doesn't permit the normalising trick.

### 1.7 Baum–Welch (EM)

Chicken-and-egg: knowing the states makes parameter estimation trivial counting; knowing the parameters makes state inference easy. EM alternates.

**E-step** — posteriors from forward-backward:
$$
\gamma_t(i)=P(q_t=i\mid x_{1:T})=\frac{\alpha_t(i)\beta_t(i)}{\sum_j\alpha_t(j)\beta_t(j)}
$$
$$
\xi_t(i,j)=P(q_t=i,q_{t+1}=j\mid x_{1:T})=\frac{\alpha_t(i)A_{ij}B_{j,x_{t+1}}\beta_{t+1}(j)}{P(x_{1:T})}
$$

**M-step** — expected counts, normalised:
$$
\pi_i=\gamma_1(i),\qquad
A_{ij}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\xi_t(i,j)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_t(i)},\qquad
B_{jk}=\frac{\sum_{t:\,x_t=k}\gamma_t(j)}{\sum_{t}\gamma_t(j)}
$$

Read these as: *expected number of $i\to j$ transitions divided by expected number of visits to $i$*. It's exactly maximum-likelihood counting, but with soft (expected) counts instead of hard ones. Monotonically increases the likelihood; converges to a **local** optimum — hence multiple restarts.

### 1.8 Summary table (for the applied question)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Hyperparameters** | number of hidden states $h$; alphabet size $d$; EM restarts; convergence tolerance; smoothing/priors on $A,B$ |
| **Learned parameters** | $A$, $B$, $\pi$ |
| **Preprocessing** | tokenise events into a finite alphabet; segment into sequences; integer-encode; merge rare symbols; handle variable lengths |
| **Interpretation** | states = behavioural/functional phases; $A$ = phase dynamics and expected dwell time; $B$ = what each phase emits; Viterbi = decoded trajectory |
| **Limitations** | must fix $h$ a priori; EM local optima; Markov assumption (no long-range memory); geometric dwell-time distribution; conditional independence of emissions; label-switching (states unidentifiable up to permutation) |

The **geometric dwell time** point is a good one to have ready: $P(\text{stay in }i\text{ for } \tau) = A_{ii}^{\tau-1}(1-A_{ii})$, which is often unrealistic — real phases have peaked duration distributions. That motivates hidden semi-Markov models.

### 1.9 Connections

- **HMM = discrete-state Kalman filter.** Same forward recursion; continuous Gaussian states instead of discrete.
- **HMM vs mixture model.** An HMM with all rows of $A$ identical is just a mixture model — the temporal structure is exactly what $A$ adds.
- **Generative vs discriminative.** HMM models $P(x,q)$; CRFs model $P(q\mid x)$ directly — Day 12 material.
- **MC one-liner:** *An HMM models sequences via a latent Markov chain emitting observations; forward computes the likelihood, Viterbi the most likely state path, Baum–Welch fits the parameters by EM.*
